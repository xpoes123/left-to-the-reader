# Maitzen's HACKENBUSH video — what we learn, what we avoid

Source: [HACKENBUSH: a window to a new world of math](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZYj4NkeGPdM) (2021) by Owen Maitzen. ~35 min, ~10,247 transcript words. Full transcript at `research/maitzen-hackenbush-transcript.txt`.

## TL;DR

Maitzen takes the *opposite* path from a textbook: starts from Hackenbush gameplay, builds intuition, and discovers Sprague–Grundy + surreal numbers + the entire CGT framework *by following what the games force on you*. Halfway through he says: *"I pulled a sneaky on ya. This video isn't actually about Hackenbush. It's about combinatorial game theory. The cute colourful pictures were just there to lure you in."* That sentence is **the same trick our game plays in Act 3**. We're aiming at the same kind of awe via different means.

Maitzen's video is the most important reference for our project. It is also the thing our players will compare us to. We need to **learn from his pedagogy, not replicate his content**.

## Pedagogical moves he uses that we should steal

1. **Build values from the simplest case.** One blue branch = 1. One red = −1. Combinations and balanced positions teach addition and zero by the hand, not by definition. We do this in Act 2 (Sketches 1–3).
2. **Let positions force you to expand your number system.** When a position appears that *can't* fit the existing values (not >, not <, not =), invent a new kind of number for it. Stars and arrows arrive this way. **This is exactly the structure of our Act 2 teaching arc** — the "stacking" / "bare sum" terminology arrives because the player needs a name for what they're doing.
3. **Use cloud-of-confused-numbers as a visual mental model.** Maitzen draws stars and arrows as clouds covering a range of confused numbers on a number line. Beautiful, immediately legible. **We should adopt this as a visual idiom in our game.** When the player encounters a position whose value is "confused with" zero, we show a small cloud over the number line on the page.
4. **The "I pulled a sneaky on ya" reveal.** Maitzen reframes the entire video at the midpoint: "this isn't about Hackenbush, it's about CGT." **Our Act 3 reveal does the same: "the library isn't 22 puzzles, it's one game."** Direct structural analogue.
5. **Show, don't tell, don't name.** Maitzen lets the math arrive before the terminology. He uses "balanced", "confused with", "cloud", "star" long before he attaches academic names. **Our in-fiction "stacking" / "bare sum" terminology serves the same purpose.** Standard names are revealed last (his at the end; ours in the optional postscript book).
6. **Show extreme range with a speed round at the end.** Maitzen's last 5 minutes are a flurry of weird stuff (on/off/dud, switches, atomic weight, tiny-one, hot vs. cold, thermographs, the Noah's Ark Theorem). The speed-round signals "there's much more here than we covered" without trying to teach all of it. **Our postscript book could play this role** for math-savvy players who want to know the names of everything.

## What he does that we should *not* replicate

1. **Don't start from Hackenbush.** Maitzen owns the "derive everything from Hackenbush" pedagogical path. If we open Act 2 with Hackenbush we'll feel derivative. We should *use* Hackenbush as one game-family among several (Nim variants, Toads-and-Frogs, Domineering), not as the spine.
2. **Don't go all the way to surreals, switches, atomic weight, loopy games.** Maitzen covers the whole CGT cosmos. We can't — we have ~22 puzzles for the whole game. **Our scope ends at "every game has a value and combined-game values stack."** Surreals, switches, hot/cold, atomic weight, thermographs — all dropped from our scope, or relegated to optional postscript material.
3. **Don't use his exact terminology.** "Star" / "up" / "down" / "confused with" / "tiny" / "over" / "under" / "on" / "off" / "dud" are *his* established names (well, Berlekamp/Conway/Guy's, popularized by Maitzen). We've chosen "the stacking" / "the bare sum" already, which is good — we should keep inventing in-fiction names rather than reach for the canonical ones until the postscript.
4. **Don't treat this as a teaching video.** Maitzen is *teaching*. We're *making a game*. Players solve, we don't lecture. Our marginalia hints; it doesn't explain. Maitzen explains beautifully; we should not try to out-explain him.

## Where our project genuinely differs

| | Maitzen | The Library |
|---|---|---|
| Medium | YouTube video | Interactive puzzle game |
| Scope | All of CGT including surreals, switches, loopy | Invariance (Act 1) + computing game values + disjunctive sum (Act 2/3) |
| Narrative | None (math explainer) | Inheritance, partnership, mystery |
| Player role | Watch and absorb | Solve puzzles, complete unfinished work |
| Theorem-naming | Eventually names everything | Never names until optional postscript |
| Source material | Hackenbush as universal lens | Curated USAMTS / Putnam / olympiad problems |
| Ending | Tribute to Conway, book recommendation | Player completes predecessor's work |

We're not Maitzen-the-game. We're closer to *Proof*-the-game-with-puzzles. The math is borrowed; the artistic frame is ours.

## Concepts from Maitzen we may want to use in artifacts

These are math-content concepts we *could* gesture at in marginalia, even if we don't formally teach them. They're things H.M. and P.W. might *correspond about* without the player having to master them:

- **"Confused with"** — a position not greater than, less than, or equal to another. Maitzen's term; we could let H.M. or P.W. use it in a margin note: *"These two positions are not equal. They are not bigger or smaller. They are... confused with each other. I do not have a better word."*
- **The cloud visualization** — when the player encounters their first nimber/up/down, the relevant book page could include a small ink-drawn cloud over the number line, hinting at the visual mental model without explanation.
- **Surreal numbers as the destination** — H.M. and P.W.'s correspondence might *gesture* at the existence of values between integers ("half-moves," "fractional positions") and even at omega-scale games (an infinite Hackenbush position), as little flares of the wider world they're exploring. The player doesn't have to solve any of these; they're worldbuilding.
- **The Conway / Berlekamp / Guy era as historical setting** — if we want to anchor the predecessor and partner in a specific time, the 1970s–2010s era (when CGT was being formalized) is the natural fit. H.M. and P.W. would have been working *alongside* the real mathematicians without quite breaking through, which is a bittersweet historical position. (Optional decision; not load-bearing.)

## One direct quote worth preserving

Near the end Maitzen says, about Conway specifically:

> *"Not only were his discoveries revolutionary and exquisitely beautiful, not only was he a trailblazer in several emerging fields of math, but he had a perfect sense of humour, and the mind of a teacher and an entertainer."*

And then about *Winning Ways*:

> *"More than anything, I think Winning Ways is the purest embodiment of the spirit of math. We have questions, about the nature of games, and through our search for answers we begin to examine some of our core assumptions, eventually getting a golden opportunity to lay out a new system of simple rules that lead to an endless cascade of chaotic consequences, far beyond the scope of our original inquiry."*

That second quote, slightly rewritten, could be the **opening line of the postscript book**. Or near it. It captures the spirit of what we're trying to make.

## Maitzen himself

He died December 2021, shortly after publishing this video. His mother wrote a tribute essay ([Novel Readings, 31 Dec 2021](https://rohanmaitzen.com/2021/12/31/owen-maitzen-1997-2021/)). The math community treats the video as a kind of testament. Our project will be compared to his work *and* there is a real ethical/emotional weight to acknowledging him.

**Recommendation:** an end-credits or dedication line that names Maitzen alongside Conway, Berlekamp, and Guy. Something like:

> *"For Owen Maitzen (1997–2021), who showed us what these games meant.
> For Elwyn Berlekamp, John Conway, and Richard Guy, who found them."*

That dedication is *also* a final layer of the game-is-a-tribute frame the project is built on — H.M. and P.W. are fictional, but the math is real, and so were the mathematicians who built it.
